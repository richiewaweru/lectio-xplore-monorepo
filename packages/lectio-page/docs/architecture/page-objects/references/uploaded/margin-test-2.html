Aside A1
Placed early. Should sit beside the opening paragraphs.

Photosynthesis is the process green plants use to convert light energy into chemical energy stored as glucose. Water is drawn up from the roots, carbon dioxide enters through pores on the underside of the leaf, and light is captured by chlorophyll held inside the chloroplasts of leaf cells.

The reaction produces glucose and releases oxygen as a by-product. That oxygen leaves the leaf through the same pores that admitted the carbon dioxide, which is why a submerged water plant under a bright lamp appears to give off a steady stream of small bubbles.

Because the process depends on light, the rate at which it happens changes with the conditions around the plant. Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, temperature and the availability of water all act as limiting factors, and the slowest of them sets the overall pace.

Aside A2
Positioned deliberately so that it falls close to a page boundary. Watch whether it is clipped, duplicated, or pushed awkwardly.

A plant kept in darkness cannot photosynthesise however well it is watered, because no light energy is available to drive the reaction. A plant in bright light with closed pores cannot photosynthesise well either, because carbon dioxide can no longer reach the interior of the leaf.

Leaves are shaped for the job they do. They are broad and flat, which presents a large surface to incoming light. They are thin, so gases have only a short distance to travel. They contain many chloroplasts near the upper surface, where the light is strongest, and they are served by veins that bring water in and carry sugars away.

Photosynthesis and respiration are often confused because they involve the same substances. Respiration runs in the opposite direction: glucose and oxygen are consumed, carbon dioxide and water are produced, and energy is released for the cell to use. Plants do both; animals do only the second.

The relationship between the two processes is what keeps the composition of the atmosphere roughly stable. Carbon dioxide exhaled by animals is taken up by plants, and oxygen released by plants is taken up by animals, in a continuous exchange usually described as the carbon-oxygen cycle.

Aside A3
A longer note, set here to test whether a tall aside near a break behaves. It should either sit fully on one page or move cleanly to the next, never split and never overlap the main column.

Most of the mass a plant gains as it grows does not come from the soil. It comes from the air. The carbon atoms in the sugars, starches and cellulose that make up the body of the plant were carbon dioxide molecules before photosynthesis captured them.

This is unintuitive and worth stating plainly, because learners commonly assume that a plant eats soil in the way an animal eats food. Soil supplies water and dissolved minerals. It does not supply the bulk of the material from which the plant is built.
