Give the learner a reason to care and a concrete situation
to hold.
A torch is switched on, shining towards a wall. A hand is held between the torch and
the wall. On the wall you can see a bright patch of light, and close to it a hand-
shaped dark patch. Look at where the bright patch and the dark patch appear.
Where is the dark patch on the wall? Before you read on, commit to where you think
the dark patch will land.
Build the idea itself. State it, ground it, make it usable.
When a torch shines on a wall, its light travels in straight lines outward from the
torch. If you hold your hand in front of the torch, the opaque hand stands in the path
of those lines, so the light that would have reached one region of the wall never gets
there. That region of the wall therefore receives no light and looks dark — this unlit
region is the shadow. A shadow is not something the hand gives off or sends out; it
is simply the place the light cannot reach because the hand is in the way. The
shadow appears on the side of the hand away from the torch, because light can only
be blocked downstream of its source.
Follow the light in the torch–hand–wall arrangement. Light leaves the torch as rays
travelling in straight lines towards the wall. The rays that meet the opaque hand stop
there, because the hand blocks the light. The rays that pass beside the hand keep
going in the same straight direction until they reach the wall. On the wall, immedi-
ately beyond the hand and away from the torch, the place where the blocked rays
would have landed stays unlit and appears as a dark region. That dark region takes
the shape of the hand standing in the beam, so its outline matches the outline of the
hand, and it sits on the far side of the hand from the torch.
Put a case and a near-miss side by side. The learner must
see the boundary of the idea, not just the idea.
Same torch, same hand, same wall — only the hand's position changes whether the light is blocked.
