  1     Two bean plants, one difference
Two bean plants kept the same way except for light grew very differently in two weeks.
These two plants came from the same batch and stood in identical pots of the same soil. Both
were watered the same amount on the same days. One stood on a sunny windowsill; its twin
stood in a dark cupboard.

After two weeks, the windowsill plant was taller, greener and carrying new leaves. The cupboard
plant was pale, short and had added no new leaves. Both plants received the same water and
the same soil. What did the windowsill plant use to build its new stem and leaves?

      Water is taken in by the roots from the soil and carried up the stem to the leaves.

      Carbon dioxide is taken in from the air through the leaves.

      Light is captured by the leaves.

      Soil supplies only water and minerals; it never supplies food.

How a plant makes its own food: light, water and air in the leaves                          Page 2 of 6
Photosynthesis is the process in which light energy captured in the leaves combines water with
carbon dioxide to make sugar, the plant's food, and to release oxygen. The water is taken in by
the roots and carried up the stem; carbon dioxide enters the leaves from the air. This sugar is
the plant's food, used for growth and as an energy source.

A complete explanation of a plant's growth must meet a clear standard. It must name light,
water and carbon dioxide as the three inputs, place the combining in the leaves, name the
product as food, a sugar, used for growth, and name the process photosynthesis.

An explanation that credits the soil with supplying food, or with supplying the material of new
growth, does not meet the standard. The soil supplies water and dissolved minerals; the sugar
the plant uses for growth is made inside the leaf, from water and carbon dioxide, using light
energy.

The bean-plant case from the opening is still waiting to be explained.

 3      Explaining the two plants — and the accounts that fail

  Key idea

  Soil supplies water and minerals only; the plant builds
  new growth from sugar made in its leaves, using carbon
  dioxide, water and light energy.

  THE SOIL ACCOUNT


  The belief             “The new stem and leaves were built from material the soil supplied; water and light only
                         helped the plant along.”

  The evidence           Both plants stood in the same soil and were watered exactly alike, yet only the windowsill
                         plant produced new growth. If soil material built the new growth, the cupboard plant
                         would have grown too.

  So                     Soil supplies water and minerals and nothing else. It does not supply food, and it does not
                         supply the material of new growth.



  QUESTION 2 · PRACTICE


  Why can identical soil and identical watering not account for the difference between the
  two plants?




The windowsill plant's leaves captured light energy. Inside those leaves, water drawn from the
roots was combined with carbon dioxide from the air, and the result was sugar. That sugar was
then used to build the taller stem, the greener leaves and the new growth that appeared.

The cupboard plant had the same soil and the same water, and carbon dioxide was present in
the air around it. Without light, its leaves could not combine water and carbon dioxide, so no
sugar was made and no new growth was built.

Carbon dioxide is not a comfort condition: its atoms become part of the sugar. Light is not one
either: it supplies the energy that drives the combining, and that energy is stored in the sugar.
If light and carbon dioxide were only comfort conditions, the cupboard plant, which had soil,
water and air, would have grown as well.

  THE OXYGEN-INTAKE ACCOUNT


  The belief             “The plant breathes in oxygen through its leaves and uses that oxygen to make its food.”

  The evidence           In bright light, the leaves of a leafy plant give out oxygen. The air around such a plant
                         therefore gains oxygen rather than losing it, which is the opposite of what the account
                         predicts.

  So                     When water and carbon dioxide are combined into sugar in the leaves, oxygen is
                         produced and released into the air. Making food gives oxygen out, not in.
